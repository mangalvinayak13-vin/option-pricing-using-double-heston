# The Double Heston Project, Explained From the Ground Up

## 1. The Big Picture

Imagine buying a coupon today that lets you buy a share of a company at a fixed price, say one hundred rupees, at any point over the next month — but only if you want to. If the share price rises to one hundred and twenty, you use the coupon and pocket the difference. If it falls to eighty, you simply throw the coupon away and lose nothing more than what you paid for it. That coupon is, in essence, a call option; the right to sell at a fixed price instead is a put option. Options like this trade by the millions every day on real exchanges, and the question this project is built around is deceptively simple: how much should that coupon cost, before anyone knows which way the share price will actually move?

The first serious answer to that question, and still the most famous one, is the Black-Scholes model from 1973. It says: if you know how volatile a stock usually is — how wildly its price swings around — then there is a single, exact formula for what the option should cost, derived from the assumption that nobody can get a free profit from trading the stock and the option against each other. It is an elegant and genuinely useful piece of mathematics. But it rests on one assumption that reality keeps breaking: Black-Scholes assumes volatility is one fixed, constant number for every option on a given stock, no matter the strike price or how far away the expiry date is.

Real markets disagree. If you look at the implied volatility — the volatility number that, if you fed it back into Black-Scholes, would reproduce the option's actual traded price — it is not flat at all. Options that protect against a big fall usually trade at a *higher* implied volatility than options that bet on a big rise, and options expiring in a month often imply a different volatility than options expiring in a year. Plotted against strike price, this pattern curves or tilts, and traders call the shape a "volatility smile" or "skew." Black-Scholes, by construction, cannot produce this shape at all — it can only draw a flat line. Something extra is needed. That something is letting volatility itself move randomly over time, instead of pinning it down as a constant. This project studies a particular way of doing that, called the Double Heston model, and is honest, in a way few such projects are, about both what it buys you and what it does not.

## 2. From Heston to Double Heston

In 1993, Steven Heston proposed giving volatility its own source of randomness. In his model, a stock's variance (the square of its volatility, loosely speaking "how much it wiggles") is not fixed; it drifts around on its own random path, but it is pulled back toward some long-run average level whenever it strays too far, the way a stretched rubber band pulls back toward its resting length. This single addition — volatility that moves but tends to come home — was enough to let the model produce a smile instead of a flat line, and it earned a place as one of the standard tools of quantitative finance.

One volatility factor, however, turns out not to be flexible enough to match everything a real options market shows at once. Short-dated options (a week or a month to expiry) and long-dated options (six months, a year) often need noticeably different-shaped smiles, and a single mean-reverting volatility process struggles to fit both simultaneously — it has too few moving parts to separately control "how much the smile bends up close" and "how much it bends up far away." Christoffersen, Heston, and Jacobs showed in 2009 that the fix is to give the model *two* independent sources of volatility randomness instead of one: a fast-moving factor that reacts and settles quickly, capturing the near-term shape, and a slow-moving factor that drifts for much longer, capturing the shape further out. This project implements exactly that idea, and calls it Double Heston.

It helps to think of it as two separate weather systems affecting the same city: a fast-moving local squall that blows through in hours, and a slow seasonal pattern that shifts over months. Both affect today's weather, but on very different timescales, and you need to track both separately to describe what is actually happening. The two volatility factors in Double Heston play exactly that role for a stock's price.

## 3. The Mathematics, Explained Gently

The project writes the behaviour of a stock's price and its two volatility factors as three linked equations. In plain text, using the project's own naming (a slow factor and a fast factor, each with five settings of its own), they read:

`dS = (r − q) × S dt + √v_slow × S dW_slow + √v_fast × S dW_fast`

In words: the stock price S changes, over a tiny instant of time dt, in two parts. The first part is a steady drift, set by the risk-free interest rate r (what a safe investment like a government bond earns) minus the dividend yield q (cash the company pays out, which pulls the price down a little when it is paid). The second and third parts are random shocks — one driven by the slow volatility factor, one by the fast one — each shock's *size* scaled by the square root of that factor's current variance. When v_slow or v_fast is high, the stock jumps around more; when it is low, the stock barely moves.

`dv_slow = kappa_slow × (theta_slow − v_slow) dt + xi_slow × √v_slow dZ_slow`
`dv_fast = kappa_fast × (theta_fast − v_fast) dt + xi_fast × √v_fast dZ_fast`

These two equations are what make the model "stochastic volatility": each variance factor is itself a random process, not a fixed number. Reading the slow one (the fast one works identically): kappa_slow is the *speed of mean reversion*, how hard the rubber band pulls variance back toward its resting level. theta_slow is that resting level, the long-run average the factor keeps drifting back toward — the term kappa_slow × (theta_slow − v_slow) is the rubber-band force itself, pulling variance up when it is below theta_slow and down when it is above. xi_slow is the *volatility of volatility*, how violently variance jitters on top of that pull; raising it makes the variance's own path rougher and spikier. Z_slow is an independent source of randomness driving that jitter.

One more setting, rho_slow (likewise rho_fast), governs how correlated the stock's own randomness is with its variance's randomness. In real markets rho is almost always negative: a sharp fall in the stock tends to arrive together with a spike in volatility — fear and falling prices together. This "leverage effect" is exactly why the smile tilts instead of staying symmetric: a negative rho makes crash protection (low-strike puts) more expensive than a symmetric bet would suggest. Finally, v0_slow and v0_fast are each factor's variance level *today*, the starting point its random path begins from. That is ten settings in all — five per factor — and they are exactly the ten adjustable settings on the project's own website.

One more piece of mathematics matters here: the Feller condition. For a single factor it states that two times kappa times theta must be strictly greater than xi squared — in plain language, the pull back toward the average has to be strong enough, relative to how jittery the variance is, that the variance can never actually touch zero (there is no such thing as negative volatility). The project's exact pricing code enforces this strictly, refusing to price at all if it is violated, because the closed-form mathematics isn't valid otherwise. Its simulator, used for sanity checks and live demonstrations, instead only reports whether the condition holds, and simply floors any simulated variance at zero if it would otherwise go negative — a standard technique called full truncation, needed because real fitted parameters on noisy market data occasionally sit right at this condition's edge.

## 4. How Prices Are Computed

Given a full set of ten settings, how does the project actually turn them into a rupee price for a specific option? There are two complementary approaches here, and the project implements both.

The first, and the one used for essentially every serious number in this report, is built around something called a characteristic function: a mathematical fingerprint of a probability distribution. Instead of describing "what is the chance the stock ends up at exactly this price," which is awkward to work with directly, the characteristic function describes the same information in a cleaner form that is much easier to compute and combine. Because the project's two volatility factors are independent, their individual fingerprints simply add together to produce the whole model's fingerprint — this is literally how the code combines the slow and fast factor into one Double Heston price. A classical 1951 result, due to Gil-Pelaez, shows how to turn that fingerprint back into the one number that matters: the probability the option finishes in the money, and from there its fair price. The one subtlety is that Heston's original 1993 formula for this fingerprint has a known flaw — for some parameter combinations a naive implementation can trip over a mathematical branch cut and silently produce a wrong number. The project uses a reformulation from a well-known 2007 paper, nicknamed the "Little Heston Trap," that sidesteps this by design, and computes the final integral with a 64-point Gauss-Laguerre quadrature — a way of approximating an area under a curve from a small, carefully chosen set of sample points rather than thousands of brute-force ones. The key routines live in the project's core pricing file, named `heston_log_characteristic_exponent`, `double_heston_characteristic_function`, and `price_double_heston_call`.

The second approach is Monte Carlo simulation: simulate many thousands of random possible futures for the stock price, following the same equations from Section 3 step by step, see what the option would have paid out at expiry in each one, and average those payoffs (adjusted for the time value of money). It is conceptually simpler but noisier and slower, and since the Feller condition can be violated on real fitted data, this code floors any simulated variance path at zero rather than enforcing the condition outright. The project uses it mainly as an independent check: on the live website, every price shown from the ten sliders is cross-checked against a 20,000-path simulation, and the two are expected, and shown, to agree within the simulation's own margin of error.

One more technical layer matters later: the project also has a second, mathematically identical implementation of the pricing formula, written so a computer can automatically differentiate it — work out instantly how the price would change if any setting moved slightly. That capability is essential for *training* the neural network calibrators described next, since training means repeatedly asking which direction to nudge the predicted settings to make the price a little more accurate, across thousands of examples at once.

## 5. Calibration

Pricing an option when you already know the ten settings is one problem. Calibration is the opposite and much harder problem: given a basket of real, observed option prices from the market on one particular day, work out which ten settings would have produced them. This matters because nobody posts their "true" volatility settings anywhere — the only thing anyone actually observes is the prices the market settled on.

The project's classical approach to this is an optimisation procedure. Start from some initial guess for the ten settings. Price every option in that day's quoted basket using the current guess, and measure how far off each modelled price is from the real traded price as a relative error, so a one-rupee miss on a ten-rupee option counts for more than the same miss on a thousand-rupee option. Square and add those errors into one overall score, then nudge the ten settings in whichever direction most reduces it, using a standard bounded, trust-region least-squares algorithm, repeating until the score stops improving. Because, as Section 7 shows, this error landscape can have broad, nearly flat valleys rather than one sharp minimum, the project does not trust a single run: for its real-market results it restarts from several different initial guesses on the same day and keeps whichever ends with the lowest score.

The real market data behind every calibration here comes from the National Stock Exchange of India's own official end-of-day settlement files — covering roughly three months of 2026 — not a live feed. The discounting rate comes from the Reserve Bank of India's own published 91-day Treasury-bill yields. The project had also planned to pull live quotes through a broker's market-data service, but that piece of code is an acknowledged, incomplete placeholder; nothing in this report was trained or tested on data from it.

It is worth being precise about where calibration actually happens. On the live site, moving the ten sliders on the "price an option" page recomputes a price instantly using the project's unchanged pricing code — but it does not re-fit those sliders against the market by itself. That fitting happens offline, ahead of time, in the research scripts, against the official NSE files, and its results are saved; the website reports those saved, verified numbers rather than running a multi-minute optimisation live.

Beyond the classical optimiser, the project also trains two neural networks to attempt the same job differently: read a whole basket of option quotes at once and output the ten settings directly, the way a trained eye might guess someone's age from a photograph instead of checking a birth certificate. A plain network is trained only to match prices. A second, physics-informed network is additionally forced, during training, to satisfy the project's own pricing equation at thousands of extra points and get the payoff exactly right at expiry — not just to copy outputs, but to internalise the rule the prices obey. This network's output layer is also built so it is mathematically impossible for it to ever propose a setting breaking the rules from Section 3: the Feller condition, the correlation limits, and the two factors' speed ordering are built into how its raw numbers convert into real settings, not checked afterward. How well each approach performs is the subject of the results that follow.

## 6. Walkthrough of My Implementation

The project's pipeline runs in one direction, from raw exchange data through to a public website, with each stage's output saved to disk before the next stage reads it. Raw NSE settlement files for the cash equity and futures-and-options markets are fetched and kept as ground truth. Research scripts then parse matched sets of option quotes for a given stock and date into a consistent form and hand those to the core pricing and calibration code from Sections 4 and 5. Every research run — a classical multi-start fit, a market-wide sweep, a comparison against a flat-volatility baseline — writes its evidence out as JSON or CSV files rather than keeping results only in memory, so every number reported downstream traces back to a specific saved file. A consolidation step gathers the checked, verified subset of those results into summary files, and the website's own data-export tooling turns that into the plain-English copy and numbers on the pages.

The website itself is a static site — plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript with no build step — offering six visual themes a visitor can switch between, each rendering from the exact same underlying content so nothing is ever said differently in one theme than another. It runs behind a small Python server that serves the static pages and answers one live request: when a visitor moves a slider on the pricing page, the server reprices the option using the project's pricing code and returns the new price, the option's Greeks (Delta, Gamma, Vega, Theta, Rho — standard risk sensitivities, each measuring how the price would change if one input moved), a volatility smile, the real listed option chain around that strike, and a 20,000-path Monte Carlo check — all computed live from the same model.

Worth being transparent about: the project contains two separately written implementations of the Double Heston formula. The one in Section 4, used for every research result here, is the canonical, carefully verified engine. The website's interactive pricing page calls a second one instead, written independently with slightly different internal names for the same ten settings but the identical mathematical formulation. They exist because the website's version was built earlier and reused, while the research's own pricing moved to a newer codebase; neither is "the real model" over the other — both implement the same mathematics from Sections 3 and 4.

Beyond the pricing page, the website walks a visitor through, in order: a home page laying out the question and a short history of the underlying mathematics; a page explaining each formula and each of the ten settings with a plain-language analogy; a results page laying out everything in Section 7, including an honest, dedicated section on where the model's parameters turned out *not* to be pinned down by the data even when its prices were accurate; and an "about" page listing the project's own stated limitations in plain terms.

## 7. Results

The single most important finding this project produced is also the most counter-intuitive one. Take sixty simulated option surfaces where the true ten settings are known in advance, and run the classical optimiser against each from several starting guesses. The resulting fitted prices are almost perfect — a tiny fraction of a rupee off. Yet when those fitted *settings* are compared back to the true settings that generated the data, the typical fit is, on average, no better than simply guessing the average value of each setting every time. Matching the prices almost exactly does not mean you have recovered the ten numbers that produced them.

The project then checked whether this was a quirk of simulation or a real feature of the model, running the same multi-start fitting against 2,400 real option surfaces from actual NSE data, with sixteen starting guesses per surface. On 99% of those surfaces, several guesses — a median of ten of sixteen — ended up tied for equally good, within ten percent of the best price fit found. Those tied-for-best parameter sets typically sit about 3.9 times farther apart, measured against the model's own normal range of variation, than two unrelated, randomly chosen parameter sets would — and on 92% of surfaces, at least one recovered setting landed right up against the edge of its allowed range. The honest conclusion: Double Heston's ten settings are often not uniquely identifiable from one day's option prices alone, even though the model fits those prices very well.

That does not mean Double Heston is a poor pricing model. Against the simplest alternative — one flat volatility number, fitted as well as possible to the same data — Double Heston's best fit produced a lower price error on every one of the 2,400 real surfaces, typically 23% less error. The model genuinely prices options better than assuming constant volatility; it is specifically *which* ten settings deserve the credit that the data often cannot answer alone.

The project also tested how well a trained neural network's learned settings generalise to days it never trained on. On eight held-out dates, its settings produced a median relative price error of about 17.4%, against the roughly 4.3% a dedicated fresh fit to that day could achieve — a model that performs well on its training days still does noticeably worse on a genuinely new one, like a student sharp on practice papers but less so on the exam's unfamiliar questions. Pricing with the network's own same-day settings beat a freshly fitted flat Black-Scholes on only about 4.8% of roughly twelve thousand stock-day combinations; carried forward one trading day, that rose to about 19.1%, suggesting its settings behave more like a slowly-changing description of a stock than a sharp same-day fit.

The physics-informed network tells a more mixed story. On real NIFTY options across ten especially volatile days, a fine-tuned version reached a median implied-volatility error of about 2.48 volatility points, against 2.98 for flat Black-Scholes, 2.63 for single-factor Heston, and 2.52 for a from-scratch Double Heston fit — best of the four on four of the ten days, in about 1.2 seconds against roughly 25 seconds for the from-scratch fit, nearly twenty-two times faster. But on a different, calmer stock over a separate window, the from-scratch optimiser beat the same network decisively, by more than four times. The honest conclusion: this shortcut is a fast, genuinely competitive alternative in some conditions, not a universal replacement for optimisation.

A smaller, controlled experiment on the home page makes the idea concrete. Two identical networks trained on the same forty-eight real Double Heston prices; one matched prices only, the other was also forced to obey the pricing equation at thousands of extra points. Tested on two thousand fresh points, the physics-informed network's error was about seven times smaller. Across a grid of over twelve hundred price points, the plain network predicted 187 genuinely impossible (effectively negative) prices; the physics-informed network predicted zero. Replaying training shows why: the plain network's impossible-price count falls for a while then climbs back up as it overfits its forty-eight training prices, while the physics-informed network's falls to zero early and stays there.

One worked example grounds this in an actual number. For a real NIFTY option — spot about ₹23,140.50, a call expiring thirty-two days later at a ₹23,150 strike — the pricer, run with its default settings rather than any fit, gives about ₹600.40, implying roughly 19.9% volatility; an independent 20,000-path Monte Carlo agrees, at about ₹597.10 ± ₹3.56. The option's actual closing price that day was about ₹370.15, implying roughly 11.4% volatility — default settings are a starting point for exploration, not a forecast, and that market was considerably calmer than the defaults assume.

Finally, several concrete bugs were found and fixed, each caught the same way: compare the model to a simpler case it is mathematically guaranteed to match, and investigate whenever it did not. One bug used the wrong interest and dividend rate for longer-dated options in several scripts, caught because a Double Heston fit deliberately flattened to behave like plain Black-Scholes should reproduce a flat-volatility price at every maturity, and quietly did not at the longer ones. A second involved the optimiser's internal bounds: limiting each of its ten internal working numbers independently did not actually limit the real settings they decode into, since several are built from combinations of more than one — discovered because a trivial flat-volatility fit beat the project's own "best" Double Heston fit on several real dates, which should be impossible if the optimiser had truly found its optimum. A third, subtler bug involved a floating-point rounding difference between two near-identical ways of representing the same time-to-expiry value, which was not truly interchangeable for an already finely balanced fit; repeated runs on identical input gave byte-identical answers, proving the issue was a data mismatch, not ordinary optimiser randomness.

## 8. Limitations and Future Improvements

Several limitations are worth stating plainly, because the project's own code and documentation state them plainly too. The model prices options assuming the stock price only ever moves continuously, in small steps — it has no mechanism for a sudden jump, the kind that can happen around an earnings announcement or an unexpected news event, so it will systematically struggle to explain option prices around those moments. It uses a single, flat interest rate for discounting every option regardless of how far away its expiry is, rather than a full, maturity-dependent yield curve. It is priced and calibrated against each day's closing settlement prices rather than live, continuously updated bid and ask quotes, so it describes where the market settled, not the narrower spread a trader would actually have been able to transact at during the day; a typical real options chain used in this project also only had seventeen of twenty possible quote slots actually filled, so some fits work with a genuinely incomplete picture of the day's prices. The live website's interactive pricing page reprices instantly from whatever ten settings a visitor chooses, but does not calibrate those settings to the market on its own — that fitting work is a separate, offline research process, and the results shown elsewhere on the site are its saved, verified output rather than something recomputed live. A planned integration with a live, continuously updating market-data service is explicitly incomplete in the code and was not used for anything reported here. The project's own research notes are also candid that the physics-informed network's retrained second version did not improve on the first, and caution against reading too much into comparing the two, since they differ by more than one planned change at once and only one run of each was completed — a genuine open question for future work rather than a settled result. Future improvements that would meaningfully extend this project include finishing the live-data integration properly (and making very clear in the interface when a number is live and when it is the last available close), training the physics-informed network on a wider range of trading conditions than the single volatile stretch it has currently been tested on most thoroughly, and completing the series of controlled comparison experiments that were planned but not all finished, so that each piece of the network's design can be credited individually rather than only as a whole.

## 9. Glossary

Option — a contract giving the right, not the obligation, to buy or sell a stock at a fixed price by a certain date.
Call / put option — the right to buy (call) or sell (put) at that fixed price.
Strike price — the fixed price written into an option contract. Spot price — a stock's current market price.
Expiry / maturity — the date by which an option must be used or it becomes worthless.
Volatility — how much a stock's price swings around over time. Implied volatility — the volatility number that, fed into a pricing formula, reproduces an option's actual traded price.
Black-Scholes model — the original 1973 pricing formula, assuming volatility is one constant number.
Stochastic volatility — volatility that itself moves randomly over time, instead of staying fixed.
Heston model — a 1993 stochastic-volatility model with one mean-reverting volatility factor.
Double Heston model — this project's model: two independent, mean-reverting volatility factors, one fast and one slow.
Mean reversion — the tendency of a quantity to drift back toward a long-run average.
Variance — volatility squared; what the model's equations are actually written in terms of.
Volatility of volatility — how much variance itself jitters on top of its mean-reverting pull.
Correlation / leverage effect — the tendency for a stock's price and its own volatility to move oppositely, a fall usually accompanied by rising volatility.
Feller condition — a condition on a variance process's settings guaranteeing it can never touch zero.
Brownian motion — the random, jittery motion underlying these equations, first observed in pollen grains and later explained by Einstein.
Characteristic function — a mathematical "fingerprint" of a probability distribution, convenient for combining and computing with.
Gil-Pelaez inversion — a 1951 technique for recovering an exact probability from a characteristic function.
Gauss-Laguerre quadrature — an efficient numerical method for approximating an integral from few sample points.
Monte Carlo simulation — estimating a result by simulating many random outcomes and averaging them.
Calibration — fitting a model's settings so its output matches observed real data, here, option prices.
Least squares — an optimisation method minimising the sum of squared errors. Loss function — the single number such a process tries to make as small as possible.
Greeks — Delta, Gamma, Vega, Theta, Rho: how an option's price changes if one input (price, Delta itself, volatility, time, rate) moves.
Volatility point — a small standard unit for measuring implied-volatility differences.
Non-identifiability — when more than one very different set of settings fits the same data equally well.
ANN — a network trained only to match given outputs, here, option prices. PINN (physics-informed neural network) — one also trained to obey the underlying pricing equation.
Overfitting — matching training data very closely at the cost of performing worse on new, unseen data.
Backtest — testing past settings or predictions against later data to see how well they held up. Held-out data — data set aside and never used during training, reserved purely for testing.
NSE — the National Stock Exchange of India, source of the real data used throughout. Bhavcopy — NSE's official end-of-day settlement price file.

## 10. Key Takeaways

A stock's volatility is not one constant number the way Black-Scholes assumes; the Double Heston model instead gives it two separate, independently moving sources of randomness, a fast one and a slow one, so it can match both near-term and longer-term option prices at once. The project's central and most important finding is that fitting real market option prices almost perfectly does not guarantee you have recovered the "true" ten settings behind them: on 99% of 2,400 real option surfaces tested, several very different parameter sets fit the prices equally well, sitting nearly four times farther apart than two unrelated parameter sets would. Despite that ambiguity, Double Heston's prices are genuinely better than assuming one flat volatility, beating a fitted flat Black-Scholes on every one of those 2,400 real surfaces, by 23% on a typical day. A physics-informed neural network, trained to also obey the model's own pricing equation rather than just copying prices, reliably avoids a specific failure mode a plain network falls into — predicting literally impossible prices — and on one genuinely volatile real stretch of the market it priced options more accurately than Black-Scholes, single-factor Heston, and a from-scratch optimiser, and roughly twenty-two times faster. That advantage is not universal, though: on a calmer stock, the slower classical optimiser still won decisively, so physics-informed learning is best understood here as a fast, competitive alternative in the right conditions, not a strictly better replacement for optimisation everywhere. Three separate, concretely diagnosed engineering bugs along the way were each caught the same way — by comparing the model to a simpler case it is mathematically guaranteed to match, and investigating every time it quietly did not. Taken together, the project's honest conclusion is less "we solved option pricing" and more "we built a genuinely better pricing model, measured exactly how much better, and discovered, carefully and rigorously, the limits of how much we can actually know about why."
